The Stars in Our Skies

This project explores different Mollweide projections of stars in the celestial sphere!


What is a Mollweide projection?...

A Mollweide projection is an equal area map that can show spherical coordinates on a 2D plane while preserving accurate proportions.


What is the celestial sphere?...

The celestial sphere is an imaginary, infinitely large hollow globe surrounding us. The earth is assumed to be stationary at the center of this sphere. When you look up at the night sky, you see half, or 180 degrees of the full celstial sphere.


How do we sap stars onto this sphere?...

Every star in the celestial sphere has a right ascension and declination. They are similar to the latitude and longitude coordinate system used on Earth. Right ascension (longitude) measures how east from the vernal equinox a star is. This dataset measures it in degrees (0-360), but right ascension is also expressed in seconds, minutes, and hours. Declination (latitude) measures the angle of a star's position north or south of the celestial equator. It is measured from -90 to 90, with a full -90 meaning the star is at the south celestial pole and vice versa.


This project seems so cool! Where did you get the data from to do this?...

The dataset used in this project comes from the Gaia Data Release 3, a catalog that contains information about 1.8 billion objects in our universe observed by the European Space Agency's Gaia satellite. However, I only used a random sample of 50,000 stars for this project, but I hope to harness the catalog's full potential in the near future!
